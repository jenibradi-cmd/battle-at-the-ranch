## PART A — Why a buyer chooses one product over six others

Amateurs ask **"which product is best?"** Great buyers ask **"which product best fills *this specific role* in my assortment, given my constraints?"** The same candle can be the right buy and the wrong buy depending on the slot. Curation is *role-casting*, not quality-ranking.

### The core mental shift
A product is never evaluated in isolation. It is evaluated against:
1. **The job it must do** (hero? margin engine? story piece? entry price? gift? traffic driver?).
2. **What's already in the assortment** (does it duplicate, or does it complete?).
3. **Your constraints** (cash, MOQ, lead time, shelf space, brand promise).

This is why a buyer will reject a *gorgeous* product ("it's beautiful, but it's not for us") and buy a plainer one. Beauty is table stakes. **Fit is the decision.**

### The 9-factor scoring rubric (the buyer's scorecard)

Score each candidate 1–5 on each factor, multiply by the weight, sum. Re-weight per category (a consumable weights margin/repeat higher; an antique weights story/uniqueness higher).

| # | Factor | What you're really asking | Default weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Brand fit / "altitude"** | Does it deepen the Stillwater feeling (consecrated ordinary time)? Would *she* believe it's us? | ×3 |
| 2 | **Role clarity** | Does it cleanly fill an open slot (hero / margin / story / entry / gift)? Or does it duplicate? | ×3 |
| 3 | **Margin** | GM% after landed cost. Can it carry its shelf space? | ×3 |
| 4 | **Story / provenance** | Is there a maker, place, process worth telling? Story = the premium and the defensibility. | ×2 |
| 5 | **Exclusivity** | Can I own it (private label / exclusive colorway / first-to-market)? Or is it on every competitor's shelf? | ×2 |
| 6 | **Repeat / loyalty** | Will she rebuy or refill? (Consumables, refills, seeds, jewelry-to-collect.) | ×2 |
| 7 | **Operational reliability** | Quality consistency, capacity, lead time, communication, sample-to-bulk fidelity. | ×2 |
| 8 | **Terms feasibility** | MOQ vs. my cash; payment terms; FOB vs. landed; returns/breakage risk. | ×2 |
| 9 | **Cross-sell / adjacency** | Does it sell *other* SKUs by completing a scene (table, bath, garden)? | ×1 |

> **The tie-breaker rule:** when two candidates score within ~10%, choose the one with **more exclusivity + better story + better terms**, in that order. Those three compound over years; a slightly prettier glaze does not.

### The five disqualifiers (auto-reject, regardless of score)
1. **On everyone's shelf already** (no exclusivity, you become a price-comparison casualty).
2. **MOQ that risks cash** (a beautiful product you can't sell through in 2 seasons is a liability).
3. **Quality variance you can't control** (samples great, bulk inconsistent = returns + reputation).
4. **No story** (if you can't say where/who/why, it's filler — and filler erodes the brand).
5. **Margin below category floor** (unless it's a deliberate traffic/loss-leader hero with a documented cross-sell case).

### Good-better-best: the buyer casts THREE products, not one
A mature buyer rarely picks a single winner for a category. They cast a **ladder** so the customer self-selects by budget and the assortment captures the full demand curve:
- **GOOD** = the accessible entry (recruits the customer; often best margin %, lower price point).
- **BETTER** = the volume workhorse (the sweet spot most people buy; the SKU you stock deepest).
- **BEST** = the credibility halo (the artisan/heritage piece that makes the whole category believable; sells slowly, justifies the others, anchors perceived value).

The "best" piece's job is often **not to sell much** — it's to make the "better" piece look like a steal and to signal taste. RH, Williams Sonoma, and Toast all do this deliberately.

### EXAMPLE 1 — Dinnerware (the "table" hero category)

**The slot:** Stillwater needs a dinnerware program. Per the blueprint this is a **good-better-best ladder**, not one buy. Open roles: a **BEST credibility piece**, a **BETTER private-label workhorse** (deep stock, owned margin), and possibly a **GOOD accessible entry**.

| | Candidate (real) | What it is | Exclusivity | Indicative cost posture | Best role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | **East Fork** (Asheville, NC) | Cult American stoneware, strong brand | **Low for you** — direct-to-consumer brand + on Faire; everyone knows it | Wholesale via Faire, ~50% off MSRP | A *competitor*, not a supplier |
| B | **Heath Ceramics** (CA) | Heritage California stoneware, design-canon | Low — iconic, widely stocked, premium | High; limited wholesale | BEST halo (if available) but pricey/hard |
| C | **Farmhouse Pottery** (Woodstock, VT) | Wheel-thrown, "America's studio pottery," small-batch | Medium — recognizable but co-brandable | Premium handmade | BEST credibility piece |
| D | **Mesa Ceramics** (Caldas, Portugal) | Modern fine stoneware factory, *explicitly built for demanding private label* | **High** — your own design/glaze, your name | Mid (EU value-quality), MOQ ~100–300/shape | **BETTER private-label workhorse** |
| E | **Bordallo Pinheiro** (Caldas, Portugal, est. 1884) | Heritage faience, decorative figural | Low — distinctive but their IP, widely sold | Mid | A *seasonal accent* buy, not a core table |
| F | **Bat Trang** (Vietnam) | Custom-glaze stoneware village | High (PL) — better MOQ economics at scale | Low FOB; MOQ higher (300–1,000) | GOOD entry tier at scale (phase 2) |
| G | **Generic Faire stoneware vendor** | Drop-ship-friendly catalog ceramics | **None** — on thousands of shelves | Keystone-ish | **Auto-reject** (disqualifier #1 + #4) |

**The buyer's read:**
- **A (East Fork) and B (Heath) are disqualified as suppliers** — they *are* the competitive set. You don't resell your aesthetic rivals; you learn from them. (Buying East Fork wholesale to resell would make Stillwater a markup middleman with zero exclusivity and a margin squeeze — disqualifier #1.)
- **G is an instant auto-reject** — no story, no exclusivity, on every boutique's shelf. This is the trap that makes a store feel generic.
- **The real decision is D vs. F for the workhorse, and C vs. B for the halo.**
- **Workhorse → D (Mesa Ceramics).** It scores highest on the weighted card *for this role*: exclusivity ×3 (your own glaze/shape = you own it), story ×4 (Caldas, "Portugal's ceramic capital," real provenance), terms ×4 (human MOQs ~100–300, EU reliability, ~60–120 day LT), margin ×4 (EU value lets you hit 55–60% at a believable retail). Bat Trang (F) wins on raw FOB cost but loses on MOQ cash-risk and longer QC ramp *at launch* — graduate to it in phase 2 when volume justifies the 300–1,000 MOQ and you have QC bandwidth.
- **Halo → C (Farmhouse Pottery)** over B (Heath): both lend credibility, but Farmhouse is more *co-brandable*, less ubiquitous, and the wheel-thrown small-batch story aligns to "slow." Heath is canon but premium, low-exclusivity, and harder to get wholesale — a great *reference*, a hard *supplier*.

**The decision:** **Cast D as the BETTER (deep PL stock, ~55–60% GM), C as the BEST (halo, low depth, ~45–50% GM), and plan F for a future GOOD tier.** Reject A, B-as-supplier, E-as-core, and G.

**The lesson:** the *best-quality* options (East Fork, Heath) were the *wrong* buys — because the role wasn't "find the best ceramic," it was "own a defensible workhorse + borrow credibility." **Exclusivity and role beat raw quality.**

### EXAMPLE 2 — Alpaca throw (story + apparel-home crossover hero)

**The slot:** one **BEST hero throw** with maximum provenance/ESG story (the "secretly buy + emotional" SKU), plus a possible **BETTER** volume throw.

| | Candidate (real) | Model | Story / ESG | Terms read | Best role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | **Awamaki** (Sacred Valley nonprofit, 9 co-ops / 174 artisans) | Women-led co-ops, natural dye, baby alpaca | **Highest** — named co-ops, nonprofit, deep narrative | Smaller batches, co-op capacity limits | **BEST hero** (story) |
| B | **Threads of Peru** | Wholesale co-op textiles, Sacred Valley | High — rural weaver villages | Wholesale program exists, curated | BEST alt / co-hero |
| C | **Sanyork Fair Trade** | Direct-from-producer fair-trade wholesale | Medium-high — fair trade, direct | **Strong wholesale terms**, bulk pricing | **BETTER volume workhorse** |
| D | **Shupaca** | Andean artisan groups, fair wages | Medium-high — community sourcing | Wholesale throws program | BETTER alt |
| E | **The Alpaca Factory** | Aggregator / "top suppliers" listings | Low-medium — broker tone | Variable | Sourcing-research tool, not a hero supplier |
| F | **Peruvian Fair Trade S.A.C.** | Fair-trade manufacturer, blankets/throws | Medium | Manufacturer terms | BETTER alt |
| G | **Generic alpaca distributor (US)** | Stock blankets, dropship | **Low/none** | Easy but undifferentiated | Auto-reject for hero |

**The buyer's read:**
- For a **BEST hero whose entire job is emotional + story + ESG**, weight **story/provenance ×4** and **exclusivity ×3** above margin. **A (Awamaki)** wins: named cooperatives, women-led, natural dye, a nonprofit structure you can put on a maker card and a hangtag. *That* is the product the customer screenshots and the press writes about. Its weakness (limited capacity) is acceptable *because a hero is intentionally scarce.*
- But you can't run a business on a capacity-limited hero alone. **For the BETTER volume throw, cast C (Sanyork)** — explicit wholesale/bulk pricing, direct-from-producer margin, fair-trade story good enough to tell, and the operational reliability to keep stock. C's slightly thinner narrative is fine because its *role* is depth and margin, not press.
- **E is a tool, not a supplier** (use it to find more candidates). **G is auto-rejected for the hero slot** — a generic throw with no story would actively undermine the category's premium.

**The decision:** **A = BEST hero (low depth, high story, ~45–55% GM), C = BETTER workhorse (deep stock, ~55–60% GM).** Keep B/D/F as vetted backups for capacity resilience.

**The lesson:** you cast **different makers for different jobs in the same category** — the storyteller for the hero, the reliable producer for the volume. Choosing only the most beautiful/ethical maker (A) for *everything* would have left you constantly out of stock; choosing only the efficient one (C) would have left the category with no soul.

### EXAMPLE 3 — The signature candle / fragrance (the margin engine + brand moat)

**The slot:** the launch candle (Phase 1, fast + low capital) AND the path to an owned signature accord (Phase 2, the moat).

| | Candidate (real) | Model | MOQ reality | Best role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | **Galimard** (Grasse, heritage) | Private-label dept, in-house perfumers, "reasonable MOQs" | Flexible-ish for Grasse | **Phase 2 signature accord** (heritage story) |
| B | **Bougie et Senteur** (Grasse, 25 yrs, *specialist in PL home fragrance & candles*) | Private-label home fragrance + candles, confidential briefs | Grasse-typical 500–1,000 | **Phase 1 launch candle** (purpose-built for exactly this) |
| C | **PCW France** (Grasse, est. 1986) | Perfumes, candles, home fragrance + ingredient supply | 500–1,000 | Phase 1 alt / ingredient partner |
| D | **US clean-beauty co-packer** | Domestic candle/body co-pack | Often 250–1,000, faster LT | Phase 1 *agility* (refills, fast restock) |
| E | **Generic library-scent candle vendor** | Pre-made scents, your label slapped on | Low | Bridge only; weak moat |
| F | **Mass private-label (Asia)** | Cheapest unit cost | High MOQ | Reject at launch (cash + story risk) |
| G | **DIY / local chandler** | Hand-poured small batch | Tiny | Test/limited-edition only |

**The buyer's read:**
- **Phase 1 launch → B (Bougie et Senteur).** When a vendor is *literally specialized in private-label home fragrance and candles in Grasse for 25 years*, they fit the exact role: credible "made in Grasse" story, candle expertise, confidential custom brief, manageable MOQ. Lower risk than building a bespoke accord on day one.
- **Layer D (US co-packer) for agility** — domestic short-lead restocks and the *refill* program (your repeat-margin engine) where fast turnaround beats French provenance.
- **Phase 2 moat → A (Galimard).** Develop the owned "stillwater accord" with a heritage Grasse house; amortize that one proprietary compound across candle/body/room/EDP (Diptyque/Le Labo logic). This is the irreproducible moat — worth the higher MOQ *once volume justifies it.*
- **E is a bridge, not a destination** (a library scent gets you live but anyone can copy it). **F/G** are wrong for launch (cash/story and capacity respectively).

**The decision:** **B for the launch candle, D for refills/agility, A for the Phase-2 owned accord.** Sequence matters as much as selection.

**The lesson:** the "which vendor" question is really **"which vendor for which phase."** A buyer sequences cash and risk: start where MOQ/story/speed fit *now*, and build the moat when volume earns it.

### EXAMPLE 4 — Silver jewelry (Celuk, Bali) — choosing *within* a cluster

**The slot:** the artisan-silver everyday hero + the Stillwater-symbol pendant (own design, produced in Celuk).

Live search confirms Celuk is a cluster of **hundreds** of workshops (and many that primarily run tourist *classes*). So the decision isn't "Bali yes/no" — it's **filtering within the cluster.**

| | Candidate type (real, Celuk) | Read |
|---|---|---|
| A | **SUNSRI** (manufacturer + gallery + factory tours) | Manufacturing-scale, structured for B2B — strong PL candidate; vet capacity & purity |
| B | Celuk Silver Village / co-op listings | Cluster gateway — use to source multiple ateliers; vet each |
| C/D | **Celuk Silver Class / Corner / "silver class" sites** | These are *experience/tourism* operations, not production partners — **auto-reject for manufacturing** |
| E–G | Independent family ateliers (found on-site / via agent) | The exclusivity goldmine — but require an agent, samples, and purity verification |

**The buyer's read:** the disqualifier here is **misreading a tourist-class operator as a manufacturer.** Several top search results (C/D) are jewelry *classes* — great for a research trip, useless as a supply partner. The buyer separates "places that teach silversmithing to tourists" from "workshops that produce at wholesale." **A (a structured manufacturer like SUNSRI)** is the safe PL anchor; **independent ateliers (E–G) via a trusted local agent** are where exclusivity and the best handwork live — but only after sampling 2–3 of the *same* design to verify consistency and assaying for silver purity (.925).

**The lesson:** within a famous cluster, the real skill is **filtering for production-capable, consistency-reliable partners** and not being seduced by the cluster's fame or its tourist-facing front doors. Always order multiples of one design to read variance before committing.

## The one-paragraph summary you can teach from

A great buyer doesn't pick the prettiest product; she **casts each open role** (hero / margin engine / story / entry / gift) with the candidate that best fits *that job* under her real constraints, then builds a **good-better-best ladder** so the customer self-selects. She auto-rejects the five disqualifiers (no exclusivity, cash-risk MOQ, uncontrollable variance, no story, sub-floor margin), breaks ties on **exclusivity → story → terms**, and **sequences** buys by phase and cash. The same product can be a brilliant buy or a foolish one depending on the slot — which is why curation is judgment, not taste alone.
